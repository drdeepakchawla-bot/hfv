How MAP is generated — bias flow against resistance

Fresh gas flows continuously into the circuit. The only way out is through the low-pass filter / expiratory limb, which carries an adjustable resistance valve. Flowing gas meeting a resistance builds a steady back-pressure — exactly the pressure version of Ohm's law. That back-pressure is the mean airway pressure that the oscillations ride on. Tighten the valve (more resistance) or raise the bias flow and the gauge needle climbs; open the valve and MAP falls.

MAP ≈ Bias flow (FGF) × Expiratory resistance (pressure = flow × resistance)

The filter is "low-pass" because it lets the slow, steady bias flow leak out to set MAP, while the fast high-frequency oscillation is too quick to escape and stays in the patient circuit.

Two ways to drive the oscillation — piston vs electromagnetic diaphragm

Piston oscillator: a motor spins a wheel; a crank pin turns rotation into linear to-and-fro piston travel, push–pulling the gas column. Stroke length sets ΔP.

Electromagnetic diaphragm: works like a loudspeaker. An alternating current in a coil sits in a magnetic field; the resulting force drives a voice-coil and flexible diaphragm in and out. The membrane's excursion sets ΔP and the AC frequency sets the oscillation rate. This is the principle of the SensorMedics 3100A/B. In both designs the defining feature is the same: expiration is active — the device pulls pressure below MAP, it does not rely on passive recoil.

piston/diaphragm displacement: x(t) = A · sin(2π f · t)
airway pressure: Paw(t) = MAP + (ΔP/2) · sin(2π f · t)

Switch the mode at the top and the rest of the circuit — bias flow, resistance, MAP, waveform — stays identical, showing the generator is interchangeable.

I:E ratio and waveform symmetry

The I:E ratio sets how the cycle time is split between the inspiratory (upward) and expiratory (downward) halves of the oscillation. At I:E 1:1 the two halves are equal, so the peak above MAP equals the trough below it (A = B). At I:E 1:2, expiration takes twice as long, so the trough is only about half as deep as the peak is high (B ≈ A/2) — exactly the asymmetry in Dräger's Figure 5-2.

trough/peak: B / A = Tᵢ / Tₑ = 1 / E (so 1:1 → A=B, 1:2 → B = ½A)

Crucially the mean stays at MAP at every ratio: the shorter, taller inspiratory area equals the longer, shallower expiratory area. A practical consequence is that a larger inspiratory ΔP is needed at 1:2 to deliver the same tidal volume as 1:1, since less time is spent moving gas inward. Clinical control map
  • ΔP (amplitude/power): main control of CO₂ removal. ↑ΔP → ↑tidal volume → ↓PaCO₂ (visible chest wiggle).
  • Frequency: 1 Hz = 60/min. On HFOV, ↑frequency usually ↑PaCO₂ (less volume per stroke).
  • MAP (bias flow × resistance): main control of oxygenation; recruits and holds lung volume.
  • Bias flow: washes CO₂ out through the filter and supplies fresh gas; also contributes to MAP.
